Why 3 minutes. Long enough for the real emergencies — find the bus times, get the one-time code, phone the person back, pay at the till. Short enough that it is not a place to sit: you cannot complete anything you would call a session in three minutes, and the countdown is on screen the whole time. Pause is visible everywhere while it runs (banner, notification, a band on all five tabs) and ends itself. It is an expiry, not a switch — a switch would need the user to remember to undo it, which is exactly the failure mode a pause feature is supposed to avoid.

03 Pause during a lock — my decision

Pause never subtracts time from a lock. It only suspends. When the three minutes expire the lock resumes with its full remaining duration — it does not quietly burn itself down while you are away. So a pause buys you a window; it does not buy you the lock.

It costs one of the two daily pauses, and it counts as the emergency pass in spirit. Which is why the tap is charged in one of two ways, depending on what the user explicitly chose:

  • Timer or Confirm lock → pause starts in one tap. The lock itself is already the ceremony; layers of friction on top of it would be theatre.
  • Challenge or Device-credential lock → pause starts only after the phrase is typed correctly. These are the modes whose stated meaning is “a decision, not a reflex”, so the pause inherits that charge. Bypassing it would have made those two modes meaningless.

Reasoning: the honest way out of a lock the user deliberately took is the emergency pass, which ends the lock and is spent for the month. Pause is the cheap, temporary, self-expiring door beside it. Both are published, both are recorded, neither pretend the lock was never taken.

05 What is real here, and what I invented

Real, and taken from the code rather than invented: the two accessibility modes and their exact wording (“Blocks any app, reads no screen. No site rules, and no uninstall guard.”); the fact that both modes block any app; the “never reads payment, banking, wallet or password apps” guarantee and its Settings home; the four lock kinds; the emergency pass as a ration that ends a lock; the “not read by Curfew” note in the app picker that is explicitly not a filter.

Invented for this mock: the pause feature itself and every number attached to it; the five-tab bar labels shown; the specific profiles and app names in the picker. I also had to guess that the pause lives on the Now screen and on the block screen rather than in Settings — see question 06.

06 What I was unsure about

Where “fast to reach” really is. I put pause on the Now screen (large, always one tap from opening the app) and on the block screen itself. If Curfew is usually reached by long-pressing the notification, the notification action is the only entry point that survives a locked phone — worth deciding.
Whether the charge is legible. Requiring the phrase during a Challenge lock makes pause slower in an emergency than the requirement asks for. The alternative — one tap always, rationed by the daily cap — is simpler and faster. I chose friction because a Challenge lock is a deliberate statement; the owner may reasonably overrule me.
Does pause stop an upcoming block from starting? I show it suspending what is running. If a schedule fires 30 seconds into a pause, my reading is that the pause wins for its remaining seconds — but that is a policy call the Rust engine has to make, not a UI call.
Uninstall guard during a pause. Not shown. I assume the guard keeps running — a pause is for getting to a browser, not for getting to the uninstall page.
Two pauses a day. Forest uses 5 minutes once a day; Unpluq 5 minutes daily. Two is at the generous end. One would be stricter and still usable.

My defensible answer, open to overrule Pause is charged only on the two lock kinds whose stated meaning is a deliberate choice (Challenge and device credential). On Timer and Confirm locks it is one tap. The trade is real: this makes pause slower in an emergency, which cuts against the “fast to reach” requirement. I chose it because a pause that quietly defeats the strongest lock Curfew has would make that lock decorative. If the owner prefers uniform speed, the fix is one line of policy: always one tap, rationed by the daily cap alone.
